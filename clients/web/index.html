Fly on Your Screen

A fly whose walking, turning and panic are not animation curves but the output of a spiking neural circuit stepped a thousand times a second. Move your cursor into the arena and it will see you coming.

Bringing the cursor near the fly drives its looming detectors. Click to swat — that is a hard, fast loom, the kind that recruits the giant fibre.

Heading bump —

EPG cells around the ellipsoid body. One bump of activity, and where it sits is the fly’s sense of which way it faces.

Steering —

DNa02, left against right. Turn rate is the difference between the two sides — excite one and the other falls away.

Threat field —

LPLC2 tiles the visual field in sectors. Each one fires when something expands in its patch of sky.

Spike raster —

Every spike in the circuit over the last two seconds, grouped by block. Amber is excitatory, blue inhibitory.

What is actually being computed

Each neuron holds a membrane voltage that leaks toward −52 mV with a 20 ms time constant. Arriving spikes add to a synaptic conductance that decays in 5 ms. Cross −45 mV and the neuron fires, resets, and sits refractory for 2.2 ms.

That gap between the two time constants matters more than it looks. A single 9 mV spike moves the membrane under 2 mV, because the synapse has mostly decayed before the membrane has finished responding. Neurons here only fire when enough inputs arrive together.

Why the fly holds a course

The ring is a genuine attractor, not a stored heading variable. Recurrent excitation among neighbouring EPG wedges keeps a bump alive; Delta7 inhibition pulled from the whole ring stops it spreading. PEN cells feed the bump back one wedge over, so driving the left or right set walks it around.

The working window is narrow. Sweeping the two forces against each other, the bump either dies within a second or floods the entire ring — the band between them is a few synapses wide.

Why it runs from you

LPLC2 pools motion radially, so an object expanding around a point drives it hard while one merely sliding past largely cancels. Its targets are DNp02, which runs, and DNp01, the giant fibre, which jumps.

The giant fibre is deliberately the harder of the two to reach. A fly that took off at every passing shadow would not eat.

Reading the instruments

When the fly is calm the bump drifts slowly and steering stays balanced. Bring the cursor in and one half of the threat field lights, the see-saw tips away from it, and the raster thickens as the descending neurons recruit.

Freeze happens too: strong bilateral looming reaches DNp09 and the fly stops dead rather than running.
